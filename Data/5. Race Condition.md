
## ⚠️ What is a **Race Condition**?

A **race condition** happens when **two or more operations run at the same time**, and the **final result depends on which one finishes first** — causing **unexpected or wrong results**.

Think of it like a race:

> If two runners (operations) are trying to reach the finish line (update data), whoever arrives first wins — and that may **break your logic** if timing changes.

---

### 🧠 Why is it bad?

Because you can’t **predict the outcome**.  
Even if your code _looks_ fine, under high load or fast threads, it may:

- Save incorrect data
    
- Skip steps
    
- Crash
    

---

### 📦 Real-World Example:

#### ✅ Scenario: Bank Transfer

Let’s say your balance is $100.

Two operations run **at the same time**:

- **Transaction A**: withdraws $60
    
- **Transaction B**: withdraws $50
    

If both read the balance at the same time:

- Each sees $100
    
- A writes new balance = $40
    
- B writes new balance = $50  
    ➡️ Final balance = **$50** (should be **$100 - 60 - 50 = -$10**)
    

One update **overwrote the other** → Race condition!

---

### 🛠️ How to Prevent Race Conditions:

1. **Locks / Mutex**: Prevent more than one process from accessing data at once.
    
2. **Atomic Operations**: Use operations that complete in one step, without interruption.
    
3. **Transactions + Isolation Levels**: In databases, use **Repeatable Read** or **Serializable**.
    
4. **Thread-Safe Code**: In C#, use `lock`, `Monitor`, or thread-safe collections.
    
5. **Optimistic Concurrency**: In some cases, check for version or timestamp before saving.
    

---

### 💬 Simple Analogy

Imagine 2 people editing a shared Google Sheet **at the same time**, and both click **Save**:

- If there’s no control, **one person’s work gets lost**.
    
- You need **coordination** (locks or checks) to avoid chaos.
